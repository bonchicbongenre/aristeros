What whatsleftisright.com does. A golf pass must keep every line true.

 1. A plan, from above, and two elevations, from the sides: cameras a and b, each
    a short line and a letter on the plan's edge, a looking from +x and b from -y
    at first. Each view shows the space within 1.55 of the middle; lengths are in
    a body's reach, about 1. Holding the ground turns all three about the middle
    together, following the pointer, so they stay a plan and two elevations of
    each other.
 2. The world holds at most one each of four directions, each on or off by its
    button and turned by dragging its head (a spin anywhere on or within its ring),
    or by the arrow keys while its button has the focus (half a degree a press):
    down and forward, arrows; a spin, turning one way about its axis; a stretch,
    pulling both ways along its axis.
    A direction within a degree of lying along, against or across one before it
    is set so.
 3. What is left is every turn and mirror that keeps each direction by its kind's
    law: an arrow, g v = v; a spin, det(g) g v = v; a stretch, g v = v or -v. With
    all on one axis it is a whole circle of turns, with or without the mirrors
    through the axis and across it; with nothing given, every turn and mirror.
 4. Where the world leaves a circle, the body takes 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 of it, by the
    buttons (the chosen one pressed), or all of it (the sign for infinity),
    turned as a lathe turns. With nothing given, 2 and 6 take an axis of the
    body's own with its mirrors, 3 a tetrahedron's turns and mirrors, 4 a cube's,
    5 an icosahedron's; infinity, a ball.
 5. The body is a fragment and every copy of it under what is left: drawn, not
    grown. The fragment is new on every visit and on "another body": a core, an
    ellipsoid each of whose semi-axes is at least a quarter longer than the next,
    holding the body's middle but never centred on it (its centre is at least a
    third of its smallest semi-axis away); two to four limbs, each an arm of three or four round cones bending one way
    as it narrows, or, one time in four, a spine, straight to a near point; and up
    to two lumps, a fin or a pod. A group of more than 6 turns and mirrors copies
    only the first two limbs, and one of 24 or more (or turned) only the first,
    with the lumps on them. Every part after the core starts inside a part before
    it, so the body is one piece. Dragging the body, or the arrow keys on the drawing,
    turns its fragment. A turned body is drawn from its exact profile about its
    axis.
 6. Its eyes: one point of the fragment, where a line toward the middle first
    meets it, and every copy of that point; as many eyes as there are turns and
    mirrors, fewer where some leave an eye where it is. An eye within 4 degrees
    of an axis or a mirror is set on it (10 while held), and on an axis wears
    that axis's glaze: two turns gold, three Longquan, four Jun splash, five
    crimson, six Jun blue. A turned body has an eye all round, or two where it is
    mirrored across its middle; the ball is all eye. No eye is wider than 0.85 of
    the part it is on. Eyes look toward the pointer, at most 55 degrees aside.
    Holding an eye carries it over the body.
 7. The tab's title is "Which way is down?". The label's second line says what is
    here; the third gives Haeckel's name for its figure (Generelle Morphologie,
    1866, vol. I, Table II and the contents of chapter XIII), its category and
    real type ("Eudipleura, Homo-Form"), for the shape as made, or, where his
    figures have no place for it, "incertae sedis". While a creature is drawn, the
    next line says what it is after, as a museum credits a copy: the manuscript, its
    date and leaf, or the author, book, year and plate, the most particular of them
    linked to where it is kept. The label gives the page's own size, read live, and
    one line.
 8. "what's right" draws, in dotted line, a plain solid whose symmetry is exactly the
    body's, after Haeckel's figures: a pyramid, a double pyramid, a regular solid,
    a three-sided pyramid; for a turned body his egg or spheroid; where he has no
    form, a ratchet's.
 9. The medium is specks carried by idealised flows, the same in every view:
    down, they fall at 0.25 a second, as snow; forward, they stream back at
    the body's own speed, at most 0.5; a spin turns body and medium together once in 12 seconds; a stretch is
    pure strain, 0.3 a second along it. With nothing given they wander. These
    rates are chosen, not measured. Motion is worked in fixed steps of 1/240 s,
    and a frame falling between two steps draws the specks, the crumbs, the spin,
    the stride and the passing ground that far between them; with reduced motion
    nothing moves.
10. The first row holds the two modes, one always pressed: "free play", where the
    visitor's world makes the body, and "find match". In find match, "another body"
    brings a foundling (and choosing find match brings one at once; back in free
    play, its fragment becomes a body of the visitor's world): a body made in a
    world the page does not draw, with parts
    of its own: each direction there with chance a half, along an axis of a frame
    of its own (three times in four) or slantwise (once in four), either way
    round; the first of a visit, down alone. The visitor's directions go off but
    down, "what's right" comes on and "what's left" goes off. It is placed while
    what the visitor's world leaves, at its parts and in its frame, is exactly its
    shape, judged when a direction is let go, not while it is held: then the label
    says "a body", gives Haeckel's name, and counts the foundlings placed this
    visit. Until then the label says "a foundling", "incertae sedis" and "Match
    its form."; beside its form, dotted, the form of what the visitor's world
    leaves is drawn dashed in the glaze, each from its group alone in the body's
    own frame, so that the two are one exactly when it is placed; the visitor's
    directions have dashed shafts and dotted buttons; a head is open where the
    foundling breaks its direction and filled where it keeps it; and a direction
    within 6 degrees of one the foundling keeps is set on it. Until it is placed,
    down has no weight on it and no ground is drawn: its own down is not shown, and
    the visitor's is a guess. Placed, the world the visitor made is its world, and
    it stands on the ground its parts give there.
11. The bodies are engraved, lit from the upper left: lines across each part's
    form, 4.5 page pixels apart, covering more of the paper as the light leaves
    it, at most 46 in 100, and a second set across them past two thirds of shade;
    an outline about a pixel wide where the surface turns away, and a line where
    two parts meet; under them a wash of the body's glaze. Without WebGL 2 the
    body is drawn in silhouette.
12. Colours come from the house tokens: paper behind each view, the body washed
    in its glaze. A dot after the theme button turns the glazes through three
    families. Eyes keep their colours in either theme. Dark mode follows the
    system until the theme button turns it for the visit; nothing is kept.
13. On a full screen the page is seen whole without scrolling: the label beneath
    the drawing, or beside it where that leaves the drawing larger, its last line
    standing on the drawing's foot.
14. A screen reader hears the world and what it leaves ("Down and forward. One
    mirror is left."), and "Placed." while a foundling is.
15. A click on the drawing, not a drag, drops a crumb there, at the middle's depth
    (at most 12). The flows carry it as they carry the specks. The body goes by its
    own strokes, which share its symmetry, so only along the directions its group
    keeps (as Curie has it): any way, within its mirror, along its axis, or not at
    all; at 0.3 a second (chosen), slowing within 0.015 of where it can come
    nearest; and only for a crumb it can get there, the nearest such. The body
    stays in the middle and the rest moves past it. A crumb within 0.02 of it is
    eaten, and a line under the label counts what is eaten this visit, beside the
    foundlings placed.
16. The favicon is two eyes, each looking out its own side. The console gives the
    Greek word for left, aristeros, and what it says: the better one. That is the
    standard account, from aristos, best, the left called the better to avert its
    omen; Beekes calls the deeper root uncertain. The 404 says nothing is left.
17. A body going forward turns for a crumb it cannot reach, or that has fallen
    behind it, since the stream outruns its stroke: the least turn that brings the
    crumb ahead of it and within reach, about down, or about the spin's or the
    stretch's axis if down is not given, or about any axis if none is. With the
    medium's directions on different axes it does not turn. It turns at 4 a second
    times the turn still to make, at most 60 degrees a second (chosen), and slows to
    turn, going at 0.5 times the cosine of that turn, at least 0.05 (chosen); its
    turning, its speed and its stroke each follow their aims smoothly, through a
    critically damped filter of 6 a second (chosen), so that none of them jumps.
    Nothing inside the circle it turns on can be reached by turning: for a turn of
    more than 30 degrees with the crumb there, it goes on straight until the stream
    has carried the crumb out to one and a half times as far from the circle's
    centre (chosen), then turns. While it turns it strokes only along the turn's
    axis, where its symmetry lets it, climbing or sinking to the crumb meanwhile. A turn about an axis its group keeps, as a spin is kept, moves
    with every turn and mirror the body has, so it carries the directions the body
    can go onto themselves and brings no crumb nearer. Every turn it makes is
    therefore one its symmetry forbids, and it bends while it turns, as much as it
    turns: each point moved toward the turn by up to half the square of its distance
    along forward (chosen). The label keeps naming the form it was made with, as through
    a stride: a turn is a pose, and Haeckel's types are of forms. A turned body,
    drawn whole, and a foundling do not turn.
18. In a world that leaves just one mirror, "another body" brings, one time in three
    (chosen), a beast from the bestiaries instead, the elephant or the hedgehog
    equally, made of the same parts as a body and mirrored: the elephant after the
    Rochester Bestiary (British Library, Royal MS 12 F XIII, f. 11v, about 1230),
    its legs straight, since the Physiologus says it has no joints in its knees,
    with the wooden tower the bestiary says the Persians and Indians fight from,
    square, of logs, on a cloth; the
    hedgehog after the Aberdeen Bestiary (MS 24, f. 24r, about 1200), with its long
    open snout and the grapes it rolled on in a row along its back, on a dome of
    spines set along its surface's outward way and tipped back toward the tail. Its mirror is set on the world's,
    its feet against down and its head forward. While the world leaves just one
    mirror it stays; otherwise the body beneath it is drawn, and it is back when the
    world is. In a world that leaves five turns and five mirrors, the same comes of
    the brittle star after Haeckel's Ophiodea plate (Kunstformen der Natur, 1904,
    plate 10), figure 1: a disc lobed between its arms, five jointed arms fringed at every
    joint with fans of spines, and the five jaws of its mouth, set on the world's
    turns and mirrors with its mouth against down. It has no eyes. Where four turns
    and four mirrors are left comes the moon jelly, Aurelia, after his Discomedusae
    plate (plate 98), seen from below: a flat bell of eight lobes fringed with 48
    tentacles, four horseshoe gonads, an eye at each of the eight notches of its
    margin, and four frilled mouth arms. Haeckel draws the arms curling one way round,
    which keeps its turns and not its mirrors; here they hang in the mirrors' planes.
    Where six of each are left comes the jellyfish Carmarina, after his Trachomedusae
    plate (plate 26), figures 1 and 7: a domed bell with a six-armed star of gonad
    leaves under it on its canals, a long stalk ending in six lips, at each canal's
    end a long tentacle coiling in its mirror's plane, and between them a short one
    arching up about the bell, set with nettle clusters. Where three of each are left
    comes the iris, after Koehler's Medizinal-Pflanzen (Gera, 1887), Iris pallida,
    Haeckel's plates having none: three falls drooping, each with its beard,
    three standards cupped upright between them, three crested style arms over the
    falls with a stamen under each, the ovary three-lobed as the plate's figure 6 cuts
    it, and the stem to its foot. Its leaves, standing in a fan, and the rhizome they
    rise from keep no three turns, and are left out. Where two of each are left comes
    the radiolarian Petalospyris dinoceras, after Haeckel's Report on the Radiolaria
    (the Challenger's, 1887), plate 87, figure 12: a nut-shaped shell, mammillate,
    a lobe either side of its sagittal ring, a spindle-shaped horn one and a half
    times the shell's length, and a flared coronet of broad truncate feet (the text's
    twenty-five to thirty, taken at 28). Where nothing is left, the flounder,
    Pleuronectes flesus, after Bloch's Oeconomische Naturgeschichte der Fische
    Deutschlands (1782-84), Taf. XLIV: on its blind side, both eyes on its eyed side
    by the dorsal edge, the dorsal fin from over the eyes to the tail and the anal fin
    shorter; it is set by the world's directions, its eyed side against down. Turned
    whole about an arrow, the flask Lagena, which is Ehrenberg's Ovulina (Kunstformen,
    plate 81), its neck ringed, its few basal teeth left out. Where a mirror across
    the axis is left too: with two turns, Ehrenberg's Stephanastrum rhombus
    (Mikrogeologie, 1854, Taf. XXXVI, fig. 33a), four arms joined in a rhomb; with
    four, Haeckel's Acanthostaurus purpurascens (Die Radiolarien, 1862, Taf. XIX),
    twenty spines in Mueller's five circles, the four of the equator long; with three,
    five or six, Heliodiscus glyphodon (Kunstformen, plate 11), a disc whose marginal
    spines, "of variable size" in the text, are taken twice as many, alternately
    large and small; turned whole, Coccodiscus lamarckii (Report on the Radiolaria,
    plate 36), a lens in chambered rings, the rings' beams left out. Where nothing is
    given: with three, Tetraplagia phaenaxonia (Report, plate 91), four spines along a
    tetrahedron's axes, their irregular branches taken regular; with four, Centrocubus
    cladostylus (Report, plate 18), thirty-two club-shaped spines from about a cube;
    with five, Circogonia icosahedra (Kunstformen, plate 1), twenty plates and twelve
    spines, the mouth on one plate left out; turned, a Sphaerozoum colony
    (Kunstformen, plate 51), its sphere of jelly alone, since only a ball keeps every
    turn and mirror. The label's types for the tetrahedron, the cube and the
    icosahedron are not figured; Haeckel's Report on the Radiolaria finds those
    ground-forms in Tetraplagia, Centrocubus and Circogonia. A creature of turns and
    mirrors is its group's copies of one piece, as a body is, but the piece is drawn
    from the plate. Where turns are left and no mirror through them, Haeckel's
    figures have no place, and the label says "incertae sedis"; four such creatures
    come all the same. With two turns, as a spin along down leaves them at two, the
    by-the-wind sailor, Velella, after Chun's Die Ctenophoren des Golfes von Neapel
    (1880), p. 15, Fig. 5, his example of the "einstrahlige Radiaten" he set against
    Haeckel's types, whose two halves are congruent and have no symmetric quarters:
    an elliptical mantle and float, the sail standing through the middle at 20
    degrees to the long axis, broad pointed flaps diagonally opposite, and beneath,
    the central polyp, the gonozooids and the marginal tentacles. Chun gives the
    sail's crest an S without saying which way it turns, and it is drawn flat. With
    four turns, the moon
    jelly as he drew it, each arm running out and curling about 66 degrees round.
    With five, the periwinkle, Vinca minor, after Thome's Flora von Deutschland,
    Oesterreich und der Schweiz (Gera, 1885), the plate numbered 542: the flower
    face up on its stalk, its tube with the sepals at its foot, the pale five-angled
    throat and the stigma in it, and five broad lobes joined at the throat, each cut
    slantwise, the broad end turned one way round as the plate's open flower has it;
    its leaves, in pairs along trailing stems, keep no five turns, and are left out.
    Which way the sail, the arms and the lobes turn is the world's (chosen): they
    trail the spin, so in the world's mirror image they turn the other way. With a half turn
    and the mirror across it, as a spin alone leaves at two, the diatom Pleurosigma
    quadratum, after W. Smith's A Synopsis of the British Diatomaceae (1853), plate
    XX, figure 204: a flat frustule, rhombic, its ends leaning opposite ways, with its
    median line and its nodules at the middle and the ends. Its two valves nest, so
    the mirror across them keeps it; turned over it is its own mirror image, and has
    no hand to follow. Where a stretch crosses the spin it lies along the stretch
    (chosen), as a tide draws a rod out. The other worlds of turns without mirrors
    through them (three or six turns about an arrow; three to six turns with
    the mirror across them; the turn through the middle alone) draw the body: no
    figure has been found that keeps exactly their turns.
19. "what's left", pressed from the first, draws, through the body's middle in every view, the directions its
    own strokes can take it: those every turn and mirror it keeps leaves where they
    are, the same space its motion is confined to. Where it keeps one mirror that is
    the mirror, drawn as a ruled square; an axis, a line; nothing, so that it is
    pinned, a ring at the middle; and where it keeps nothing, every way, so every
    view is ruled. Beneath it, dashed, are the spaces a few of the turns and mirrors
    it keeps leave free, each a mirror's plane or a turn's axis, taken until where
    they all meet is that space, each the most nearly square to those before that
    the body has (two mirrors of a fourfold body square, of a threefold at 60
    degrees): a pinned body shows them crossing at its middle. A
    turn-and-mirror, or the turn through the middle, leaves only the middle free,
    and has no space to draw. From each crumb a dotted line runs to its foot there,
    where the body heads. A crumb is dropped on the plane through the middle square to the view
    clicked, and that plane is drawn as it lands, fading over 1.2 seconds (chosen).
20. While the views are turned off square, "square" stands in the plan's corner and
    turns them back, over 0.6 seconds (chosen): the plan looking along down where
    down is given, else along the axis of the most turns the body keeps, and its
    right along forward where forward is given, else along a mirror, so that b sees
    that mirror face-on.
21. Where down is given it is weight. A body with a wing (a thin blade) flies, and
    goes as above. Else a body whose lowest point is on a limb has legs, and jumps;
    else it walks. The elephant walks, as elephants cannot jump and the Physiologus
    gives it no knees; the hedgehog jumps; the brittle star, the flounder and the
    foraminifer walk; the medusae swim and the radiolarians, the colony, the diatom
    and the sailor drift, as fliers do. The iris and the periwinkle are rooted: they never
    go, turn or leap,
    in any world, and eat what reaches them; with forward alone the stream goes past
    them, as a river past a reed. A jumper, a walker or a rooted creature stands on the ground
    through its lowest point, drawn ruled, moving under it as it goes: it strokes
    only across down, and not while it turns. What falls to the ground lies there,
    and moves with the ground; snow that reaches it is gone. A crumb clicked under the
    ground falls to it from the height of the body's middle, straight above where it
    was clicked (chosen). It eats what lies on
    the ground once it is over it: once the line up from the crumb comes within 0.02
    of one of its parts. A jumper leaps for a crumb still
    falling that is over it and within 1.2 (chosen), rising to the crumb's height
    and falling back under a weight of 4 a second each second (chosen).
22. A beast on its ground walks at the speed of the ground under it: a trot, left
    fore with right hind and right fore with left hind, half a stride apart, each leg
    swung whole about its hip by up to 0.35 radians, about 20 degrees (chosen), the
    elephant's on legs without knees. At almost every moment the walking beast keeps
    no mirror; the mirror and half a stride together keep its walk, and the label
    names that. At rest its legs hang straight. The creatures of turns and mirrors
    have no legs, and glide.
23. When a direction is turned and the world's symmetry grows (its order rises,
    the same directions given), the turns and mirrors it now keeps flash once in
    every view, in the glaze, fading over 1.2 seconds (chosen): its turn axes as
    lines, its mirrors as their planes, at most 12, or the ring where it keeps only
    the turn through the middle. Giving or taking away a direction does not flash.
    The line under the label counts the forms the visitor's world has taken this
    visit, from the second on.
